## Shared Welcome & Core Objectives
*(Additional framing for both syllabi. HCC departmental objectives remain in place; this sits alongside them. UH text is the primary source, lightly adapted.)*

### Shared Welcome (core paragraph)

This course is intended to help you as a citizen or resident of the United States to understand the reasons behind the Constitutional structure and politics of the American system. We will examine basic questions such as “What is government?”, “What are the basic jobs of government?”, “What limits should be placed on government?”, and “What are good forms of government?” We will approach these questions critically, based on the common goal of living together as peaceful members of a society of dignified equals.

Starting with the ideas and history that shaped the founding documents and an examination of those documents themselves, we will look at how the American experiment contributed to this goal at the outset, how initial shortcomings have been addressed, and what improvements (if any) remain.

This course contains material that relates to current local, state, national, and international political controversies. Respect for the opinions of others and civil discussion are the standards for our work together. Respectful disagreement is encouraged.

### Course-specific one-line variants

**HCC 2305 (Federal Government)**  
…understand the reasons behind the Constitutional structure and politics of the **national** government of the United States.

**UH 2306 (US and Texas Constitution and Politics)**  
…understand the reasons behind the Constitutional structure and politics of the American system **and the State of Texas**.

### Shared Student Learning Outcomes (instructor framing)

By the end of the course, students should be able to:

1. Apply critical thinking skills to the problem of having a government effective enough to secure rights but limited enough to prevent tyranny.
2. Apply critical thinking skills to the problem of how best to live together as members of a peaceful society of dignified equals.
3. Explain the basic problems that arise from the unique, defining quality of government (its legitimate monopoly on coercive force).
4. Explain the main institutional solutions to those problems found in the United States founding documents and how those solutions advanced (or began the movement toward) a society of dignified equals.
5. Evaluate how later changes in the constitutional and political system have furthered or hindered that goal.
6. Distinguish constitutional politics (near-consensus, rule-setting) from ordinary politics (majority decision-making within those rules).
7. Assess trade-offs and unintended consequences in concrete policy and institutional choices.

### Optional UH-only addition (for Texas)

8. Compare key features of the United States and Texas constitutions and explain how federalism shapes policy and political incentives in practice.

### Optional short “What this course is really about” blurb (either syllabus)

Government touches every aspect of our lives for good or ill. American government often seems to do things—or fail to do them—in ways that make little sense. This course is designed to lift some of that veil: much of what looks dysfunctional is the predictable result of institutions deliberately built to limit power and force compromise. The central practical question running through the semester is how we can live together in a peaceful society of dignified equals.
